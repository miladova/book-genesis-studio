# Chapter 3 — Visiting Hours

The flowers began on the fifth day, which was how the building knew that Berlin had been informed.

They came up the German corridor in the arms of orderlies who had been told to be careful of them, and they were absurd and sumptuous in the frost-light, hothouse carnations and hothouse carnations' expensive relations, a colour of red the hospital had not seen since a Christmas bazaar in 1938. By nine there were two vases at the window bed. By ten there were four, and a tin of chocolates with a silk ribbon, and a visiting card engraved in copperplate that lay propped against the vase like a small tombstone. The German wing, which Politeness visited the way weather visits a coast, had been visited before; it had never been *rained on*.

Sylwia watched the morning's traffic from her own corridor, through the double doors, with a sheaf of the day's lists in her hand as pretext. A colonel of the Governor's staff came first, young and correct, with a face like a closed ledger and an adjutant to carry his glove; he stood at the foot of the bed for four minutes, said perhaps forty words, and left a card. Then an SS-Gruppenführer's physician, who came to look at the wound, not the man, and was received by Vogel with the correctness of one craftsman inspecting another's bench, and who spent a long time, in theCORRIDOR afterwards, saying to Vogel with real feeling, "A colostomy on a Rittmeister," in the tone other men used for *a Vermeer on a bicycle.* Then, at eleven, the one that mattered.

The old man came on a stick of black wood, one-armed, the empty sleeve of a general's greatcoat pinned and looped with the economy of long habit, and he came slowly, and he came alone, without adjutant or flowers or card, and the ward's German orderlies — who saluted everyone — saluted him twice. He stood at the window bed a long time. From the doors, through the glass, Sylwia could see only his back and the bed's foot: the pinned sleeve, the stick, the blanket's stillness. He did not speak loudly, wherever he had been speaking; when he turned to go there were tears on the old cheeks, freely worn, like campaign ribbons, and he said something to Ilse on his way out, and Ilse put her hand briefly over her mouth, and he went down the corridor tapping the black stick before the orderlies' eyes, which had gone anywhere rather than his face.

"Oberst von Rehdorf," Ilse said later, at the doors, unprompted, in the flat voice nurses keep for facts they have already had to say once to themselves. "Retired. He commanded the regiment. In Poland he lost the arm and in France the son." She looked at the carnations as though the carnations had asked her something. "He came in his own car from Pankow. He said —" The German sister stopped, and chose, and delivered the sentence exactly: "He said, *tell him the regiment is still his to lose.*"

The morning's second lesson followed the first. She stood at the double doors with her lists and counted the day's callers the way she counted sponges, because her whole life had trained her to notice what was absent from a field. Rank had come: a staff, a physician, a regiment's old father. Blood had not. No one had come who shared his name, no telegram was read out over the linen, no family car was parked below with its engine ticking in the frost; and the absence had a shape she knew from her own house — the shape of a family that had spent its money, years ago, on a different account. It was not her business. She made it her observation, filed it at the back of the ledger where the unexplained entries lived, and went down to her ward.

Halina intercepted her at the hypnotics cupboard with the particular brightness that meant an opinion had been marinating since breakfast.

"So. The Polish side of the building is asked to believe," Halina said, "that four rooms of hothouse carnations are for *a German.* Janek has been sent to carry water for the vases. Janek, whom they shot through the leg in September of thirty-nine on this side of the same street, is now the florist's apprentice to the occupation." She slammed the little drawer of the cupboard with the violence of the righteous and lowered her voice to the needle it kept for the actual point: "What did he do to get himself a regiment, a general, and the flower budget of the Große Pause? Is his family the Kaiser?"

"His family is Berlin," Sylwia said. "Close enough."

"And you cut him open and stand there while they throw flowers. Does the professor know? Does anyone over here know why *you* of all people are on that chart?"

"The chart knows. You may trust the chart."

"I trust you," Halina said, with the directness that was the most expensive thing she owned, and went off down the corridor with the morphine tray, and left Sylwia at the cupboard with the keys in her hand, feeling the exact weight of the trust — its purity, its crank, its firing pin — settle against her palm like the little brass key to the drawer at her desk.

The corridor absorbed it. Somewhere below, the war went on issuing its timetables; the trams rang on Wolska; the frost kept its grip on the drainpipes; and in the ward the flowers went on being alive at the expense of some hothouse's coal, imported colour, imported scent, the empire's whole apparatus of meaning arranged at the bedside of one unconscious cavalryman from Uhlandstraße, and she stood in the doorway with her lists and understood, in the body, what a name was worth in the century's ledger: everything, and nothing, depending on the page.

At noon Ilse came to find her at the Polish dispensary counter, where she was arguing a gram of caffeine into existence against a requisition form, and said, with the careful neutrality of a diplomat reading a note that may be war or may be weather: "He is awake, Herr Doktor. Properly. He asked questions."

"What questions?"

"Where he is. How long. Whether the foot is his." Ilse counted them off; a sister relays a pulse. "And who held the knife."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That a Polish surgeon operated." Ilse's face did not move. "The chart says so. He read it twice." A pause, in which the dispensary's ammonia held its breath. "He said: *then she is still doing it.*"

"Doing what?"

"I assumed surgery," Ilse said, and went back up her corridor, and Sylwia stood at the counter with the requisition form unsigned in her hand, hearing, from a gymnasium infirmary thirteen years dead, steel give way like ice under a heel.

* * *

She went up at three, at the afternoon's turning, when the visiting dignitaries had been burned off by visiting hours' ordinary traffic and the ward belonged again to its trade. She had prepared herself on the stairs the way she prepared for an abdomen: instruments counted, surfaces swabbed, the plan of the encounter laid out in the mind's theatre. The plan was a single sentence: *He is a case.* Everything else — the name, the alley of years between the name and now, the flowers, the voice about to arrive through the ward's air after thirteen years of silence — all of it was to be kept outside the doors like a coat not permitted in the theatre.

She gave him the anaesthetic she had prepared: formality.

He was awake. That was the first fact, and it arrived with a force the charts had not let her feel: awake, and watching the door as she came in, the way prisoners and cats watch doors, without seeming to. Propacity up a little, the grey receding from the lips; the bed's machinery — flask, drain, the colostomy's discreet appliance — arranged around him like arguments. His eyes found her at four metres and did not perform surprise; whatever performance the moment wanted had been conducted already, in the night, with two counted fingers and a chart.

She took the wrist because it was the work. The pulse beat under her fingers, quick and light, the debt's interest still accruing, and she noted it, and hated the small warmth of relief in her own chest, and noted that too, the way one notes a murmur.

"Rittmeister," she said, in German. "You are in the Wolska hospital. You were brought off the mined transport four days ago. Your chest and abdomen were opened the first night; the abdomen again, two nights later. The drain and the intestinal appliance are doing their work; in some weeks, if the fistula stays closed, the appliance goes and the gut is rejoined. You will be bored before you are well. Boredom is the correct treatment." She had the chart up as she said it, pen making its lines, the whole of it delivered to the ward's air, official, level, the voice she used for education: the dose, the times, the prices. She did not look at him while she said it, because the looking had to be spent carefully, and there was a great deal of looking on hand.

"Kotarska," he said.

Four syllables. All of them present, each in its place, the K like a latch, the stress where the stress belongs — and she had not heard the name said correctly out of that mouth in twenty years. It had always been *Kotowska,* the vowel flattened by his tongue's habit or his father's table or the whole grammar school's convenience, the little daily amputation that had taught her, more efficiently than any politics, what a majority could do: it could decline to carry your name. And now the man lay with a hole sewn through his middle and said the name whole, correctly, without ceremony, the way one reads a chart aloud, and the correction went up her arm like current, like a hand closing on the wrist — not gratitude; she would not owe him the gratitude — more the jolt of a lock she had believed rusted shut turning easily, once, in a stranger's hand.

She examined him with the same official air, and he submitted to it, and the submission had a quality she recognized and distrusted: the stillness of men who have been inspected before, professionally, by experts, in worse rooms. Lungs: the right base still muffled, the drain's whisper adequate. The wound: clean. Heart: regular. He bore her hands on his chest the way he had once borne Cloos's corrections, from under the lids, missing nothing, and when she came to the appliance he watched the check with an attention so exactly like a fencer's reading of an opponent's blade that she heard the gymnasium in it and moved on quickly.

"The morning's morphia," Ilse noted at the end of the round, "he has declined. Twice. He says his head is for his own use."

"His head may keep itself until the dressing at six," Sylwia said. "At six it takes the morphia, whether it approves or not. Pain that is fought inflames everything around it, Rittmeister — that is arithmetic, not comfort. You were a cavalryman; you know what a horse does to a field when it fights the rein. Fight the rein at six. The rest of the day is your own."

The ghost of something crossed his face that on another man would have been called amusement. "You have a gift," he said, "for prescribing to the cavalry, Doktorin."

"And you for taking it like garrison orders. We are both of us overqualified for this bed."

Ilse, at the flask, poured the oil of the professional sister's tact over the exchange and looked away, and Sylwia set down the chart, and it was then, at the door of the round, that the other thing surfaced, the day's first dry card, played without looking up:

"Who sent the garden?"

"Your regiment sent the flowers. Rank sent the rest. Rank sends flowers; families, in my experience, send telegrams." She had not meant to say the last part aloud. It was out before the surgeon could countermand it, a piece of her own curiosity dressed as information, and she saw it land — saw the half-second of stillness, the eyes going to the carnations and away, the jaw taking the small weight of whatever the telegram's absence meant — and he gave her nothing else, only: "Then the ward may keep the flowers. They are doing the dying for everyone."

"You pronounce it," she said, to the chart.

"I had four days," he said, "and nothing else to read."

The ward breathed around them. She changed the dressing with Danuta's hands under hers and Ilse's flask keeping its tempo, and the wound underneath was clean — clean, she let herself think, clean as this month of February had any business making it — the drainage respectable, the line of her first suturing knitted into him like a signature into wet ink. Under the fresh dressing, on the left flank, the old scar sat pale in its new country of shaved and painted skin, and Ilse, folding the soiled linen, asked the question sisters ask over old scars, professionally, as one asks about a coin found in a garden.

"The old one, Herr Doktor — it is well closed. Years old. Steel?"

"Thirteen years," she said. "An examination."

Ilse, who had expected *a duel,* perhaps, or *shrapnel '16,* received *an examination* with the folded politeness of her order, and carried the linen out, and the ward was theirs again, the two of them and the machinery, and he said, with his eyes half shut, spending words like a man counting out the last coins of a currency:

"The examiner passed out."

"The examiner was fourteen years old and has improved since," she said. "The examinee fainted in the infirmary before the third stitch, which the record will show, since the record's witness is still alive."

"Your father stitched it."

It was not a question, and the bottom of the ward dropped away for a moment, the way a tram drops at a broken point, and righted itself. She put the last strip of tape down with two fingers.

"My father stitched everything in that district for thirty years. It is not evidence of acquaintance."

"No," he said, and closed his eyes, and the silence stood a moment at the bedside like a third visitor, and left.

She washed her hands at the basin and gave him, over the sound of the water, in the same educating voice, the appliance's administration: the bottles, the emptying, the dignity of the routine. He listened the way he had once listened to Cloos's fencing lectures, from under the lids, motionless, missing nothing, and at the end he said: "Then I am in your care again."

"You are in the ward's care. I am the consultant of record."

"The consultant of record," he repeated, like a man fitting a key he has been handed, "held the case when there was no record and no ward to consult." The eyes opened; dark, annoyed at their own weakness, the boy's eyes grown to fit the officer. "Charts are written by the living, Schwester. I will want mine accurate."

"The chart is accurate," she said, and gathered her instruments, and at the door — because the plan had not provided for the door, and the door did what doors do, turned her back to him for the length of a breath — his voice came after her, dry, level, the voice of a man laying one card face up on a large table:

"Thank you for the nights, Doktorin. Both of them."

She did not turn around. She walked out through the double doors with her instruments held the way relics are held, level, unhurried, and only in the corridor did the breath she had been carrying since noon come out of her, slowly, through the nose, in the licensed quiet of the broom-cupboard passage, where the mop buckets lived and no one counted faces.

*Thirteen years,* said the gymnasium's high windows, in her blood, where she kept it. Very well. Since the door had opened it, since the name had come up out of its grave pronounced whole: thirteen years ago.

* * *

March of 1930, and the gymnasium smelted of resin and wet overcoats and the liniment the fencing master used on his own shoulder, an old Saxon called Cloos with wire-rimmed spectacles and a voice like a train being given instructions. It was the winter exhibition, the school's annual parade of its virtues before its patrons, and the card that year said, in Cloos's upright hand: *Sabre — Schoeler (VIIa) v. Kotarska (guest, Sokołverein Moabit).* A girl's name on the card was the evening's curiosity; half of Dahllem had come to see whether the Polish club's girl was a phenomenon or a joke, and the gallery sat in the rows with their breath showing, and among the boys in the second row — she saw him when she walked out, and never afterward could unsee him — sat Reiner Wedekind, loud even in silence, waiting to be entertained.

She was nineteen, and wore the Sokoł club's spare jacket, cut for a boy, too big at the shoulder, and she had been taught by Pan Brzozowski, who had vouched for her with Cloos across a bottle of the club's terrible coffee, in the grammar of a man who had fought in 1920 and believed that the sport's whole dignity was that it did not care what was written on the church rolls of anyone's city. *You will lose,* he had told her, kindly, on the tram out. *It does not matter. Show the mask where the lesson lives.* And then, as the tram turned into the school's street: *But if he grows impatient — and they always grow impatient, the tall ones, the eldest sons — go to the wrist, and then go deep.*

The bout went to five. She took the first hit off the start-line, her attack arriving before his civility had, a touch on the wrist that rang through the hall and stopped the whispering as if a hand had been laid over a bell. He adjusted. That was the thing about him that the school saw and the school applauded — the patience, the reading of an opponent like a difficult text, no wasted motion, no visible anger at the wrist — and he took three in a row, patiently, all of them earned, the last to her shoulder with a rider's time and weight, and the gallery woke up and applauded its own kind, and somewhere in the applause a voice — Wedekind's, there was no need to look — called out something with *Polin* in it, and was shushed by a master, and laughed low among the boys.

Three–one. At the guard line he said, quietly, under the hall's noise, courteous as an usher: "You fence like a lesson." And when she looked at him: "Textbook. Brzozowski's textbook. I have fenced the page." It was perfectly pitched to travel no farther than her mask, and it did its work; it went in under the jacket like the point itself, and the fourth hit was his, 4–1, and Pan Brzozowski at the wall closed his eyes in the manner of a man memorizing a disappointment.

She won the fifth with the stop-hit he had not read, because it was not in any textbook, being pure temper — the time-hit in preparation, arriving in the same half-second as his launch, both lights on the electrical box, and Cloos's hand giving it to her, 4–2, and the hall's surprise made a sound like wind in a stack of papers. Then 4–3. Then the long one, the rally that the gallery would talk about for a season, blades meeting and meeting, her legs beginning to shake and the temper gone cool and bright as the cold of a tap, his patience against her fury, the whole gymnasium narrowed to the metronome of the exchange — and at the end of it the lunge, her gamble, everything down the corridor of the blade in fourth, and his parry meeting it with the full pleasant weight of a seventeen-year-old cavalryman's certainty.

The blade broke at the forte.

It broke the way ice gives under a heel — no crack she heard so much as a change in the floor of the world — and the stump of it, driven by her whole spent body behind it, went past his displaced guard and under his left arm, into the flank below the ribs.

For half a second — the half-second she had kept in a drawer of herself for thirteen years, the drawer painted shut, the drawer now standing open in a Warsaw ward with a mop bucket for company — for half a second, before the blood arrived, what she felt was joy. White, entire, arc-lit joy: the century's whole ledger in balance, the slate's edge avenged, the boy who had broken Stefan's name in the schoolyard — who was *Stefan's* senior, *Stefan's* type, the type that dared smaller boys onto ice — pinned on the point of her arm like a specimen. Joy, and then the blood came through the white jacket in a line, and his face changed — surprise first, the surprise of a boy meeting arithmetic — and Liesl Schoeler, twelve years old at the end of the row with the housemaid's hand around hers, began to scream, and the scream took the hall the way fire takes a curtain, and the joy turned to ash so fast there was no transition, no border post, no place her soul could have been shown turning it in.

The school doctor was at a birthday in Grunewald and unreachable. Cloos, grey under the wire rims, sent the porter running the three streets to the Lindenstraße clinic for the Polish physician — protocol in that district had been broken for six years and everyone knew whom you fetched for steel — and so it was her father who came, in the February dark, pannier under his arm, and knelt on the gymnasium floor in front of the son of Konrad Schoeler, and opened the boy's jacket with scissors, and did his work. She stood in the infirmary's corner, still in the borrowed jacket, sweat going cold on her, and watched her father's hands: the probe, the little system of the forceps, the six sutures, even thread, even line, the hands that had once saved the boy's mother's life going about their oldest argument, which was with death, and which had never once in thirty years inquired of the patient's name.

Konrad Schoeler came at the second stitch, in evening dress, straight from some tribunal of the republic's money; and the two men — the judge and the doctor, the benches of whose one shared history were a verdict and a ruined clinic — stood on either side of the boy for ten minutes and spoke, so far as she could hear across the infirmary's smell, of nothing. At the end Konrad said, with the full weight of his courtesy on the word: *Herr Kollege. I am grateful.* And her father, tying off, not looking up, said: "The needle does not take sides, Herr Rat. It is the one of us that never has."

In the tram home neither of them spoke. At the table her mother stood up with her face already going, and her father washed his hands, and dried them, and said to his daughter the only sentence the evening was ever permitted to leave behind: "The hand will heal. So will he. You I cannot stitch."

Ludwig Schoeler was back at school in eleven days, and the scar began its second career. She heard the phrases secondhand, the way one hears weather from a far room: *the Polin tried to kill me; you should see it; medal enough for the east front when my time comes* — the wound converted, with the school's applause, into his currency; her shame spent, stamped, circulated, legal tender in every corridor of the Realgymnasium. She never fenced there again. Pan Brzozowski sent no message. At the Sokoł hall the next week she trained in the empty ring at six in the morning, alone with the masks and the morning, and no one ever asked, and she never told anyone — not Brzozowski, not Halina, not the man who lay on the bed with her thread in his belly — not anyone in thirteen years — that the half-second had happened at all.

* * *

Vogel's office was on the German wing's quiet side, and it was the one room in the hospital that had been allowed to keep a personality: models stood along the sill above the radiator, iron bridges and a viaduct, the works of a man who built the Württemberg railways in white pinewood at one to two hundred and thirty, all winter, at night, in occupied Warsaw. He was gluing a truss when she came in, the tweezers in his surgeon's fingers, and he finished the joint before he looked up — a courtesy, or a tactic; with Vogel the two were welded.

"Doktor Kotarska. Sit. You will take schnapps." It was not a question; the bottle was already poured, a medicinally small one. "You drank with me in thirty-nine when we closed the typhus annex. The bottle remembers."

"I remember the typhus annex," she said, and sat, and did not take the glass, and Vogel, having registered the refusal in whatever ledger he kept, drank her share himself and set the glass down on a railway timetable.

"A family matter," he said, "has become an imperial matter. I have had a call from the medical inspectorate — the Rittmeister's file is known in two ministries; there is an uncle —" the tweezers moved, absolving, "the connections are Berlin-wide. And the inspectorate asks, in the polite way such places ask, why a Polish surgeon's name stands daily on the chart of this particular officer. I am asked to regularize the case. Wendt will take over the management. Your consultation will be noted, in writing, with gratitude — the inspectorate has been generous with the word."

"The appliance is four days old," Sylwia said. "The fistula is quiet but it is a door, not a wall; it wants watching by the hands that built it. The drain comes out this week; the man is already training on the appliance, badly, and he will take correction for the next fortnight from one person only, and that person is the one who has stood where he stands. Handover kills, Herr Oberstabsarzt. It is the best-established number in the whole of medicine, and it is established at every bed where a case changes hands because a ministry found the bed interesting."

"You mistake me," Vogel said, mildly, laying down the tweezers. "I did not invent the inspectorate's interest. I am the wire it came down."

"You are also the chief of this wing, and the wing is where the number will be paid." She kept her voice at the level she used for teaching, and heard, behind it, the other voice, the one that had spoken from the gymnasium's drawer; and she moved over it, laid a dressing on it, kept it out of the field. "Give the case to Wendt and you may record a gratitude. Keep it with me and you may record, in six weeks, a convalescent officer fit for a staff in Berlin. I do not need the consultation noted. I need the outcome unambiguous. You did not build those bridges by letting a junior pour the concrete at the span."

Vogel looked at her for a long moment. The radiator ticked; the little viaduct held its span above the heat, nothing on it high enough to fall. His suspicion — the wing knew it; suspicion was the ward's weather — was shaped, this particular suspicion, like respect: he had watched her hands for three years the way a man who builds things watches anything that holds weight, and he had never once caught the hands out, and it offended some German severity in him that he had not.

"You understand," he said, slowly, "how it can be made to look. Berlin will read your name on that chart as propaganda — yours, or our eccentricity. There are men in this city who collect irregularities; you have seen the collection grow."

"I am a surgeon. What Berlin reads is Berlin's literacy problem."

"Answer me one thing, then, as a surgeon." He folded his hands. The question, when it came, was not the one the room had been built for. "Why does it matter to you? Truly. Not the number for number's sake — I have my own numbers and they argue both ways. You have carried this case past two jurisdictions and you are sitting in my office refusing my schnapps to carry it past a third. A consultant could be forgiven — would be *relieved* — to hand a German staff officer's belly to a German staff officer's knife. Why are you here?"

The honest answer stood up inside her, dressed and ready, a sentence she had not invited: *because he is mine.* Because the case had come up out of the rails into her hands, and the hands had no country, and the body under the drapes had been hers to hold — three seconds, then three hours, then three nights — and she did not let go of what she held; her whole life was a refusal to let go; the ledger, the lock of hair, the glove, the carbon slip, the fists of her own history, held, held, held. And underneath that sentence, one storey down, a darker floor that she did not open: *because no one else will ever be as careful with him; because no one else has had as long a training in his exact construction; because I have known the architecture of that body since it was seventeen years old and I am the only surviving expert in it, and if it dies on any other table I will never forgive the profession that let it.*

"The case is mine," she said. "It was made mine on the rails at twenty to eleven, by the man whose signature your inspectorate honours. I am asking you not to spend my competence on politics. It is the only thing the war has left me that I brought to the job."

Vogel considered her over the viaduct. Then he took a form from the drawer — the occupation's true sacrament — and filled it in against the timetable, and initialed it, and held it out.

"Continuity of management, until the appliance is removed or the fistula closes. Weekly summary in German, to me, Thursdays. And Doktor Kotarska —" the wires of his decency showing for a moment through the uniform, the thing that made him unbearable and safe in the same hour, "if this goes wrong, the gratitude of the inspectorate will find other words. Keep it from going wrong. I have grown attached to the number."

She took the form. On the shelf above the desk, in a silver frame gone dull, stood a photograph she had never let herself study before and studied now, because the room had given her a quarter-minute in which not to study it would have been the studied insult: a boy of perhaps seventeen in the brown of the Labor Service, spade shouldered, grin wide under a cap a size too proud. The frame had no black ribbon. The ribbon, in this city's grammar, would have been a mercy. It had instead a woman's handwriting on the glass's margin, small, washed out, patient: *Weihnachten 1942.* Somewhere a mother had written the year on the glass as if the glass might argue, and the boy had gone east to the Volga in the New Year with his year written on him, and Vogel came in every morning and built bridges at one to two hundred and thirty, where nothing falls.

Vogel watched her look, and let her look, and neither of them said anything about it, and in the language of that corridor the silence was the whole conversation, two professionals agreeing that the currency in the room was too heavy to count aloud.

In the corridor outside, the flowers at the window bed had begun, in the stale heat, the first faint dishonest sweetness of their dying; the visiting card still stood propped against the vase, copperplate to the room. She went down her own stairs with the form in her pocket and her hands quiet, and at the landing, between the broom-cupboard passage and the dispensary, she stopped for exactly as long as it took to conduct the audit she had promised herself on the stairs: *why.*

*Because he is mine.* The sentence stood where it had stood in Vogel's office, patient, uniformed, awaiting inspection. She looked at it with a specialist's eye and declined, this once, to probe for the second wound beneath — the way she had once, in Poznań, declined a consultant's offer to reopen an old belly on a cold morning: the case was stable; the floor beneath the sentence was load-bearing; and she was, she told herself, climbing back into the ward's noise, far too busy a surgeon to go digging under the load-bearing walls of her own house in February.

The weekly summary, now, was due on Thursdays, in German, to a German — her own hand, her own grammar, the occupier's language performing the case's progress: *wound healing by first intention, condition improving.* Improving. She climbed the stairs into the ward's noise and understood that she had just agreed, in a chief's office, to write that word every Thursday about a man whose improvement she had, on the rails, three seconds at a time, once been the only thing standing between and nothing: that she had fought a ministry for the right to say *improving* in German over Ludwig Schoeler's bed, weekly, in writing, under her own name — and that some clerk in two ministries would file the word, and some schoolboy forty years from now might read it in an archive, and none of them, none of them, would ever know what it had cost the hand that wrote it.

She put the form in the desk drawer, on the honest side, face up. Then, after a moment, she moved it to the other side, under the calendar, with the rest of the archive. It was, after all, an account of debts.
