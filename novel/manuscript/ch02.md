# Chapter 2 — The Name

Warsaw came up out of the snow that morning the way it always did in February, a city of chimneys deciding whether to forgive the day. From the ward windows the smoke went up in pillars, unmoving, as if the air had been starched; the trams ran their German timetables along Wolska with a ringing she could hear even up here, and under the ringing, always, the horses, the army's horses, because the army had come east on rails and horseflesh and was now, ticket by ticket, coming back west on both.

The ward woke around her. Sister Bogumiła had the night's report folded in one hand and the Blessed Sacrament's economy in the other — she managed both with the same faces — and the report was good: the typhus annex quiet, the little Wolska boy's fracture riding straight, the aged priest's heart keeping its own disagreeable time but keeping it. Porridge was issued at seven. The sheets were counted, because the Germans counted sheets, and the lice were inspected for, because Poland counted lice, and between the two accountancies the patients were visited, which was the work.

Wierzbicki came at half past eight, leaning on the cane and the past in equal measure. He was seventy and had been famous everywhere surgery was spoken of, and the occupation had reduced him to a textbook the Germans tolerated because the textbook was in Latin and the man was useful; the gout had him by the right foot and the times by the throat, and he had answered both the same way, by shortening his step. He rounded the ward with his hands clasped behind him like a man attending his own trial, and where she was he stopped, and did not look at the chart, and looked at her.

"Janina tells me the night held forty-one," he said. Janina was a fiction; his informants were legion. "And that you took the belly."

"The German wing's table was engaged."

"Mm," said Wierzbicki, and moved on to the old priest, and that was all of it, the whole lecture, delivered in a syllable. She had been his resident for six years and his colleague for three and she knew the syllable's grammar the way she knew the frost: something had been said that would not be said again. At the door, on his cane, he added, in the voice he used for teaching, which was the voice he used for everything, "In September of thirty-nine we all of us operated on them, doktoro, the whole city, and it kept us alive. Then it stopped being our table. There are reasons it stopped. Do not make it yours." And he was gone, cane-tapping, down the corridor where the morning had already begun to need him.

She did her rounds, and the ward did its work around her, and the morning took its coins. The old priest asked, as he asked every day, whether he might be given back his trousers, and was told, as he was told every day, that the trousers were being mended, and blessed her anyway, because they both knew where the trousers were — on a German ward, on another man's legs' worth of requisition — and blessing was the one currency the occupation had not learned to inflate. The Wolska boy, seven, pinned from hip to ribs, asked her the question the whole city asked under other plaster: whether the war would come to his street. Not yet, she said, and both of them believed her, because the alternative to believing her was unlivable, and medicine was in that sense like the church, trading in the survivable fiction.

At half past ten the linen count came up short by four sheets and Sister Bogumiła spent twenty minutes making the corridor's accountancy sorry it had been born; at eleven the radio in the German passage began its midday march music, and everyone on the Polish side developed, simultaneously and without conspiracy, a need to be somewhere with a door.

She went up to the German corridor at noon, because the numbers were hers now whether the corridor was or not: Ilse's night chart held out to her in the doorway like a plate of something the sister was not sure was edible. Temperature 38.2 at four, 38.6 at six. Pulse 112, then 118. Abdomen: *somewhat distended, tympanitic.* Drain output: serous, a faint green breath at the edge of the dressing, which might be nothing, which might be the liver weeping, which might be — she put the thought down like a hot instrument. The distal colon's loop sat in its dressing like a poor relation, pink and apologetic, doing its new work.

"He slept," Ilse said. "He said nothing when he woke in the night. Some of them weep, in the night, the young ones, but not this one. He counted his own fingers."

"He what?"

"When I changed the drip." Ilse looked, for a moment, embarrassed by the whole German language. "He held up his hand and counted the fingers. Twice. Then he slept."

Alive, then, all the way down; a man who woke in the night and took inventory. She heard herself filing that away, and disliked the clerkishness of it, and filed it anyway.

The trouble came at noon, in the form of Stabsarzt Wendt, who had not appeared at the bedside all night and now arrived at it trailed by two orderlies and his own cologne. Wendt was forty-five, a Silesian with a gymnast's carriage and a dentist's fingers, and the German wing's second knife; he had been at Tannenberg's memorial celebrations in his youth and at every medical conference since, and his competence was real and his vanity had never once let it rest. He read the chart standing, all of it, though it was her chart, her knots, her night in it — read it the way a man reads another man's letters.

"A competent notation," he said, to the chart.

"Thank you," she said, to him, and watched the sentence land where it would.

Vogel came at two and did the thing she had known since dawn he would do, the thing the building had been waiting for him to do: he gathered the case into the German wing's jurisdiction with a standing order, spoken aloud in the corridor for the record of everyone standing in it. "The Rittmeister is a German case. Polish consultation ceases when the Polish stitches cease. If the abdomen reopens, Wendt opens it." A pause, moneyed, deliberate. "We are grateful for your night's work, Doktor Kotarska. It will be noted."

"Noted where?" she asked, pleasantly, and Vogel's face declined to answer, and the order stood, and she went down to her own corridor with the words walking beside her like a man paid to follow her.

She took the measure of the two of them on the stairs, because a surgeon took a pulse wherever she found one. Vogel she had read three years ago and never had cause to re-read: a decent man inside the machine, which is to say a man who would do you a kindness where the machine allowed a kindness, and feed you to it the moment it asked, and sign afterwards with a steady hand, and half mean his regret. Wendt was a different instrument and a worse one, because Wendt was not the machine; Wendt was only himself, all the way down: vanity with a medical degree, a man who could hesitate at three in the morning between a patient and his signature, and who would hate anyone who saw it done. She had not seen it yet. But she could hear the pause coming, somewhere down the corridor of the next forty-eight hours, the way you hear a train on a far bridge at night, without believing it has anything to do with you.

She wrote her evening note on the Polish side and her consultation line on the German side, and read the numbers once more at nine, and did not like them, and said nothing. 38.9. The abdomen's silence was the wrong kind now; it had stopped being the silence of healing and started being the silence of held breath. Nine years of bellies had taught her the difference the way a fisherman is taught weather, in the bones, before the mind signs off on it. Something in there had not finished dying.

At nine she went home through the dark city, because it was Thursday and the flat wanted its other life led. The tram was the German concession, blue and white, and it carried Wola westward toward the centre in a silence like held snow: the occupiers in the front seats with their collars open to their own evening, the city behind them, sitting the way Poles now sat on trams, folded around some interior document. Along Okopowa the wall ran beside the tramline, three metres of brick with the watch-towers' light lying along it, and everyone in the tram looked at it and no one in the tram looked at it; the eye had learned to do both at once, the way the mind had learned to hold two ledgers. At the corner where the cemetery wall met the Ghetto wall she got down and walked the last stretch through the frozen streets, market bag in hand, a woman of the city going home, nothing here, nothing to see. The flat on Kredytowa, when she reached it, was cold as a chapel and just as furnished with the dead. She lit the stove, ate soup from the pot, read two pages of a French surgeon's memoir of 1870, and went back to the hospital early, because the numbers at nine had not liked her either.

She slept in the duty room that night, on the cot with the army blanket, because the German corridor had a smell she did not yet have a name for and she wanted to be in the building when it spoke.

* * *

It spoke at twenty to three.

The porter first, feet on the stairs, then Janek's knuckles on the duty-room door with the particular knock he saved for emergencies, three soft and one hard, a telegraph of fear. She was dressed — she had slept in most of her clothes, in the way of surgeons in war — and was on the German corridor inside ninety seconds, coat over nightdress, hair pinned by one pin and the century.

The German corridor at night had a different acoustics from hers: thicker, quieter, the sleep of men who had learned to sleep in the mass. Sister Ilse met her at the door with the lamp carried low, and behind her, in the bad air of the room by the window, the night had already begun arranging its furniture. The oxygen bottle breathed its small machine-breath. Frost stood in the window glass an inch deep. And the man in the bed was no longer lying in the way the resting lie; he was lying in the way the sinking lie, absolutely still, spending nothing, a spender at the end of his account.

The room by the window was hot and smelled wrong. Not pus yet; before pus, worse than pus: a sweetish, sly, faecal breath under the carbolic, the smell of the abdomen turning from a held territory into a lost one. He lay grey under the lamp Ilse had rigged, the oxygen mask fogging in small defeated bursts, and his belly — she had the blanket off before her coat was — was board again, silent, and two finger-widths bigger than yesterday's. The drain's bottle held what it should not hold. His pulse under her fingers was the weasel again, running itself out.

"Time?"

"Two-forty," Ilse said.

"Get Wendt. Say abdomen, say now." And to the night orderly: "Theatre two. Warm it, lamp on, and tell them to boil everything once and boil it twice."

Wendt came at three-thirty, from the casino, in an evening's smoothness under his coat, and she watched the whole catastrophe of him arrive: the cologne losing to the ward's smell, the eyes going to the chart because the chart was safer than the bed, the pause — the pause was the thing, the considered, professional pause of a man weighing his skin against his signature, and while he weighed, the boy's — the man's — pulse spent itself against her two fingers at a rate she did not say aloud.

"Oberstabsarzt Vogel is asleep in Żoliborz," Wendt said at last. "In an hour it will be light enough to telephone —"

"In an hour he will be past surgery." She said it level, to Wendt, in German, in front of the night nurse and the orderly and God; the abdomen's door was closing as they stood there, and every sentence spent on jurisdiction was a sentence the blood paid for. "There is a perforation in that gut that I have been listening for since yesterday noon, and it has found its voice. The order says German cases are opened by German surgeons. Then open him, Herr Stabsarzt."

Wendt looked at the bed. She saw him see it — the grey, the distension, the fogging mask — and she saw the arithmetic finish in him, the terrible private arithmetic of a vain man's fear, and she could have wept for the profession, and did not, and would not.

"I observed the case at eleven," he said. "I consider the abdomen should be reopened. For the record." And then, the sentence that would travel four years and arrive somewhere she was not: "Beginnen Sie."

She began.

The theatre held the same lamp, the same basins, the same twenty-year-old quiet that rooms of cutting keep whatever the uniforms of the century. Danuta had come in for the boil-twice trays with her coat over her nightdress and her face already scrubbed of sleep; Ilse took the flask; and Wendt stationed himself by the wall in the position Germany reserved for observing officers, hands behind his back, cologne going down before the ether's tide.

Her hands went through their opening on rails: sheaf, towel, skin, line. The old incision came apart in the wound's own seam, the body cooperating in its second ruin, and the abdomen beneath was a night's work already spent against them. She stood a moment letting the picture resolve — the difference between the abdomen she had closed two nights ago and this one was the difference between a library and a flood — and put her hand in, and read with the fingers first, as always: loops, omentum, the liver's repaired edge holding its mattress sutures like a field holding its fences. There. High on the ileum, forty centimetres past the first signature, the omentum had built its little white federation across a second hole, and the federation had a sloughing floor and a trickle running under it into the general dark. She isolated it with fingers and gauze, got the loops packed away north and south, and had the hole between clamps while Danuta held the light's angle and the room smelled of ether and wet clay.

The abdomen, reopened, told its story at once. The omentum had spent the day doing valiant paperwork, walling off a second hole the first splinter had punched higher in the ileum — a splinter prints twice, the way a bad typeface prints through the page, and she had known it and been overruled by geography: the hole had sat where her first operation's light had not reached. Now the wall had failed, and below the failing the peritoneum was coming up like a flooded street, grey-red, slick with the beginnings of the thing that kills at this stage of a war's surgery. They washed him. Litres of warm saline, in and out, bowls of it coming up the colour of thin clay, four bowls, six, nine, until the returning water ran clean enough to argue about, and through all of it the pulse counted itself down at the head of the table and Ilse's voice gave the numbers like a woman reading thermometer weather.

She closed the ileum's second mouth. The repair sat high and angry and would need a fortnight of luck, and luck was not a thing she could prescribe. What she could do she did: drains, dependent, two of them, to give the infection's geography somewhere to go; powder, sparingly; and then, as the peritoneum came together over the work, the pressure went.

It went like a bank holiday. One minute the numbers were bad but in a language she spoke; the next, above the draped field where Ilse kept her numbers, the weasel pulse thinned to a thread and then to a vibration, and the mask's fogging slowed as though the lungs had begun to doubt the arrangement, and the whole room — Danuta's hands stopped in the bowl, Ilse's finger on the flask, Wendt's cologne — came to a point of silence she knew from maybe six nights in her whole life, the held breath of a room in which a life stands on a coin's edge and the coin is spinning.

"Blood," she said. "All of it you have. Fast."

The third flask went in. Ilse squeezed the bulb with both hands, and the room waited on the pharmacology of a stranger's red cells and the stubbornness of one heart, and in that waiting — because her mind had always gone where it went when the instruments were out of her hands and nothing remained but witness — Sylwia stood in a courtroom in June of 1924.

The walnut benches. Her mother's glove, white, new-ish, bought for the trial, the only new thing that year, the fingers of it opening and closing on the bag's clasp through the whole of the reading, click, click, click, a tiny metronome of hope. The judge's voice from the bench going over the facts in a language made entirely of furniture — *the court establishes, the court cannot establish, the court is constrained* — and the constraining going on and on, the great soft wheel of procedure turning above them, doing its work, which was not justice and not mercy but turning. Her father standing to receive the verdict the way one receives weather. And outside the courtroom's high window, in the courtyard where the young men waited, a face at the glass: the boy, fourteen, mouthing something at her through the pane with the bottomless cruelty of the young, two syllables over and over — *Kotowska, Kotowska* — the wrong name, the flayed name, the name with its spine broken, and her own hands on the bench in front of her, and the bench's walnut worn smooth by decades of other people's ruin.

The coin was still spinning. On the table the heart, offered strangers' blood at speed and the whole of her attention, was either going to take the trade or not, and the room had stopped pretending to be a room; it was a held note.

Take, said the heart, or said the blood, or said whatever it is that decides these things in the dark under the lamp — and the thread thickened. The vibration found a beat. The beat found the next one. Ilse said the number aloud, a number with a future in it, and the room let its breath out all together, twenty lungs at once, the war's one honest exhalation.

"Numbers," she said, to no one, because the hands wanted their ritual and the hands would get it.

"Four packs, six sponges," Danuta said. "Knife, forceps, complete."

She closed him for the second time in twenty-nine hours. Wendt stood by the wall with his face the colour of the sheets, and when she stripped her gloves and held them out, palm up, the little gesture of a surgeon asking for the chart, he flinched — actually flinched, the Silesian vanity's whole architecture showing for a blink through the plaster — and came, and took the pen she gave him, and stood over the chart.

"Write what you ordered," she said. "I will sign that I held the instruments. That is what happened, is it not, Herr Stabsarzt?"

Wendt wrote. The pen made a sound like a mouse in a wall. He wrote *abdomen reopened on my order at 03.50, Dr. Kotarska assisting,* and signed it in a hand that had been taught copperplate by the Kaiser's schools, and she signed beneath, *assisted,* and the two signatures lay together on the line like two countries on a small map, and she picked up her coat from the radiator and went to find tea, because the shake had started somewhere behind her knees and the tea was the only ceremony she had for it.

So. She walked down the two flights with the inventory running, because the ledger did not keep itself: a broken standing order, witnessed by a night nurse, an orderly, a scrub nurse who was Polish and could be made to say anything under the right pressure, and a Stabsarzt whose signature now hung on her nail like a coat she could fetch whenever the weather required. Vogel would read the chart by eight and know the shape of what had happened, however Wendt told it, because Vogel read wounds the way she did, from the drainage backwards. There would be a conversation. There might be a lull in consultations, a cold fortnight, a Polish patient who would have been referred and would not be. She entered each cost in its column and let the columns sit; the sum would be presented to her in due course, by the occupying power, which never failed to invoice.

In the changing room, with the tea scalding her palms through the glass, she sat on the bench under the coat pegs and shook, quietly, professionally, the way a struck bell shakes after the strike, and let it, because there was no one to see and the body insisted on its bookkeeping. Her father's trial had lasted four hours. The verdict had taken eleven minutes to read. She had been thirteen, and had believed, going in, that the walnut wheel would grind out a true thing; and it had ground out a *constrained* thing; and the lesson of the walnut — that procedure is the machine the strong run the weak through — had been the lodestone of her whole compass since. Tonight she had broken a procedure to put a German's gut back together, and had made her break itself procedural, witnessed, signed, filed. The wheel could turn. She had learned from the best. She drank the tea.

* * *

Her father came on the Thursday, as he always did, with his cane and his pannier and the forged pass that said he was a pharmaceutical supplier's inspector, sixty-six years old and lying his way across a city whose bridges were counted by the men he was lying to. He came to the diet kitchen because it was the one room in the hospital where Polish tea could be drunk sitting down, and he came every week, and the weekly coming was the whole of their geography of safety: father and daughter, one hour, one table, the parcel between them like a neutral border state.

His own practice, the one the walnut bench had left him, travelled in that pannier too, though neither of them called it a practice and the pannier's lining told no tales to casual search: insulin for a diabetic child in Ochota drawn against the clinic's phantom stockbook, a suture kit for a midwife in a cellar parish, the names of two men in Kielce who could set a femur on a kitchen table. He had been a physician for forty years and was now, in the occupying power's nomenclature, an illegal; he wore the illegality the way he had once worn his white coat, lightly, over the same old discipline. If the occupation had made her father a smuggler of medicine, it had at least failed to make him a smuggler of fear: he crossed the city twice a week with the face of a man going to check a boiler.

The parcel this week held six eggs, wrapped one by one in newspaper from 1938 — the eagle on the masthead gazing up at her from the shell of breakfast — and a lemon, whole, unwaxed, impossible, a piece of another planet. She looked at the lemon. She looked at him. Her father had the long Kotarski face that in repose looked like a man listening for something on a far bench of an orchestra, and the hands of a man who had been inside more abdomens, human and animal, noble and poor, than anyone she had ever met, and that now shook on the cold mornings in a way he pretended was the cane's fault.

"Hospitals," he said, settling onto the stool with the sigh of the grudgingly old, "smell of the same six things in every country I have been shown. Carbolic, ether, cabbage, iron, wet wool, and fear. Yours has added a seventh." He sniffed the air of the diet kitchen with professional disapproval. "Schnapps. A German officer has been drinking somewhere below us in the night."

"A Stabsarzt," she said. "He was restrained by his duties from remaining sober."

Her father buttered the grey bread — butter being another planet's embassy — and began, as he did every week, on the ward: the old priest, the Wolska boy, Sister Bogumiła's opinions, the economy of eggs. The economy of eggs deserved its ministry: they were four zloty the piece with a ration card and a lie, eight without, twelve if the farmer had seen the color of your papers, and every household in the city had become a small bourse in protein. Her father traded in them the way other men traded in salt: he knew which farms on the Młociny road still sold to doctors, which wives of which German clerks would swap sugar for a consultation, and the eggs in the newspaper from 1938 had come to him by a chain of such knowledges, four links long, each link a favor that somebody somewhere would one day call in. The occupation had made the whole city a hospital and the whole hospital a market, and the two of them sat over their tea in the middle of it like two clerks of an old firm, taking the week's accounts. She answered in kind, the weekly liturgy, and somewhere between the priest's heart and the egg count she heard herself arrive at the sentence she had been carrying since Tuesday night like a cup filled to the brim.

"We had a man in off the trains," she said. "The mined one. Forty-one stretchers. I held the case."

"Mm," her father said, over the butter. "And is the case held?"

"It is." The bread crumb, the grey crumb, sat on the board between them. "His name is Schoeler."

Her father's hands did not stop. That was the terrible discipline of him: the butter went on being buttered, evenly, to the corner, and folded, and laid on the bread. But everything else in him stopped — the listening face, the far bench of the orchestra, whatever he had been hearing all these years that the rest of them had mistaken for old age — and the room, the little diet kitchen with its seven smells, went as quiet as the room she had operated in at four in the morning, the same held note, the coin upright on its edge.

"Schoeler," he said at last, in the tone of a man confirming a delivery. "There were two of them in my waiting room when I was a young man. Both tall. Both chess." He looked up. "Which one is it?"

"The younger. Ludwig."

"Ah." Her father put down the knife. He aligned it with the board's edge, precisely, the way he aligned everything, the way the occupation's forms aligned themselves against his signature. "I had him in ten moves," he said. "That June. You were too young to be told the position. I had him in ten — the eleventh would have taken the roof off his bishop's house, and then the queen, and I confess I was looking forward to it, Konrad losing always argued so beautifully about God. We left the board standing with black to move." He picked up his tea, and drank, and set it down. "Nineteen years black to move. Somewhere in Charlottenburg a bishop is still standing on its square wondering what the delay is."

"Dad —"

"You will tell me nothing further," her father said, not unkindly, which was the most frightening register his voice owned, "and I will ask you nothing. I am too old for history, doktoro, and too well supplied with it already. You held the case; hold it. A belly does not care whose son it is wearing." He turned his glass a quarter-circle on the table, a man aligning his one small property with the room. The diet kitchen's clock, which had stopped in the first September and had never been worth repairing, stood at nine minutes to five over the stove, and in its stopped face her father seemed, for a moment, to be reading something — a position, an old position, played out to its quiet ruin on another board entirely — and she knew, the way she had known since she was sixteen, that the thing neither of them said had a name, and that the name had been spoken in this family for the last time in February of 1927, and that the silence around it was the load-bearing wall of the house. She could have asked him now. The question sat in her like a stitch that wants picking. *Did you ever hear, Dad, what happened at the canal — what really happened, from anyone?* Sixteen years of the family grammar said no. She drank her tea and let the wall stand.

He stood, and took his cane, and wound his scarf, and became, in the little doorway, with the snow-light behind him, the exact measure of what the century had left of her family: one old man, one forged pass, one lemon of another planet's harvest. At the door he stopped, half-turned, and for one moment the listening face had something else on it, an old thing, unsurfaced since the house went quiet.

"And Sylwia." Not doktoro. "Whatever you are carrying up those stairs — and you are carrying something; I have watched you carry it since the trains — set it down before you go in to him. All of it. The body on the table gets the surgeon, or gets nothing. That is not forgiveness. It is the work."

Then the cane went tap, tap, tap, down the diet kitchen's corridor, and the forged pass earned its keep again at the gate, and she stood a while with the lemon in her hands, smelling another planet.

Her mother had worn a white glove to the verdict — it was in the desk now with the rest of the archive, a kid glove no bigger than a bird, the family's whole costume of hope kept in a drawer. Her mother had believed the verdict was a door that would open the right way if you came to it dressed. Her mother had never once, in all the years after, said the name at the bottom of the house's silence. Perhaps the glove had been for that, too: something to hold instead.

Upstairs, the German corridor at noon was sunless and bright with frost-light, and the ward breathed. She stood at the window-end of the corridor with the fresh chart in her hand — the second chart, the forty-eight-hour chart, blank above the printed columns — and filled in what was hers to fill in: the consultation line, the operations' dates, the new note, *abdomen washed, second perforation closed, condition grave but defined.* And at the head of the page, in the box for the case's name, she wrote the name herself, in her own hand, though the sister could have written it and the clerk would have copied it: Ludwig Schoeler, Rittmeister.

Four syllables. A town square of a name, granite at the corners, a name built to be shouted across courtyards and carved over doors. Her pen did not hesitate, and she stood a moment looking at the little ink fact of it, the man reduced at last to something she could hold — four syllables, a pulse, a hole in his gut plugged with her needlework and other men's blood — and she could not have said, if the court had constrained her to say, whether what she felt, standing over the name with the pen still warm, was the whole of her hatred coming home, or its first crack.

Ilse, passing with the morning's flask, offered the day's numbers — pulse, temperature, the night's small victories — and asked, with the round, enclosed politeness of her nursing order, whether the doctor wished to take the pulse herself.

"No need," Sylwia said. "Your chart will do."

It was the first time in nine years she had declined to put her own fingers on a number, and she heard the sentence go out of her and knew it for exactly what it was: the counterweight. Last time she had held his wrist because the night made it proper; this time she kept her hands at her sides for the same reason a reformed drinker walks on the far side of the street. The body had its appetites. Hatred, her father could have told her — did tell her, once, in the language of chess — is an appetite like any other, and feeds nearest to home.

She put the pen away. Down the corridor, under the frost ferns, the bed by the window breathed on, slow and expensive, and she went back down her own stairs to her own ward, where the work was Polish and the light was the same light, and the lemon sat on the office shelf doing its silent, impossible yellow best all afternoon to be another planet in a room whose planet this was.
