# Chapter 1 — Triage

The war came up from the rails at eleven minutes past ten, on forty-one stretchers carried by men who were frightened of it.

Sylwia Kotarska heard it before she understood it — the snow-silence of the Wolska hospital at night was never silence exactly, it was a low coal-smoke hum with the barracks piano two streets off and the frost ticking in the pipes, and into this came a new note, iron on iron, the couplers of a train being shoved along the siding. Then boots on the stairwell, the hospital's only true clock. Boots meant orders. Orders at that hour meant the admission hall, and the admission hall at that hour meant blood.

She was in the ward office with the month's charts and a candle stub doing the work of a lamp, and she sat for exactly as long as it took to finish the sentence she was writing — *wound contracted, no discharge, dressing changed* — because a surgeon who leaves a sentence half-written leaves other things half-written too. Then she capped her pen, put the candle out with two fingers, and went down into the cold with the ward's keys swinging at her hip like a pendulum that kept a different time from her watch.

The hall had been a chapel once, back when the Wolska hospital belonged to a God who took patients. The vaulting was still there overhead, and the niche where a saint had stood was now a cupboard for bedpans; the Germans had put their electric lamps along the walls on brackets, and under them the trestles were going up in rows, and the stretchers were coming in through the great doors two at a time, snow still on the blankets, a smell of frost and gangrene and horse — the smell the eastern front sent west ahead of itself the way weather sends wind.

Vogel stood in the middle of it in his overcoat with the collar up and his surgical apron on underneath, a thickset Württemberger with a red face made redder by the cold, holding the tally book of the transport against his chest like a hymnal. Forty-one. A hospital train off the rails east of Warsaw, a mine under the front bogie, eight hours of shunting and triage at a barn station and now this: their wreckage, decanted into her chapel.

The hall had seen this before and the walls knew it. In the first September, in the days when the stretchers had come from the other direction, she had learned to read a convoy the way farmers read a sky, and what forty-one stretchers said was the same thing stretchers always said, in any language, in either direction: that somewhere east of the city a quantitative amount of the world's youth had been converted, in an instant, into work. Eight hours ago these had been soldiers. Now they were freight with pulses, and the war would spend the night asking her to say which pulses were worth the freight of keeping. Nobody in the hall said any of this. The Germans had their tally book; she had her grammar; the frost ticked in the pipes, keeping its own minutes.

"Doktor Kotarska." He did not look at her when he said it; he was reading his book. The German wing had two surgeons that night and one of them, Wendt, was already elbow-deep in a gunner's belly in the theatre upstairs, which meant that the arithmetic had been done before she was woken, and done in German. "You will take the abdominal."

"Show me."

He showed her. The sorting had a grammar all its own and she had learned it in September of '39 and never unlearned it: you walked the rows and you read, first with the eyes and then with the hands. A sergeant with half a jaw gone sat propped against a wall holding his own helmet, patient as furniture, and would keep for hours; a boy of nineteen with a dressed knee kept shouting that he was dying and would keep for days; two Polish railwaymen from the siding stood back against the door with the deference of men who have already been sorted by rank and nation and found to be luggage — one of them with his foot crushed inside its boot by a coupler that had jumped, and already going grey at the lips, and no one's book had a line for him. She caught Janek's eye across the hall and tilted her chin at the boot, and Janek, who had been shot through his own thigh in '39 and walked with the memory of it, took the man's arm without being told twice. That was one for later, and later was a country whose border moved every night. She walked on.

Then the silent one.

He lay on the fourth trestle in the third row, and she had passed the row once already, because silence sorts low in this grammar: the shouting boy takes precedence in the minds of orderlies, the bleeder takes precedence, the one who talks about his mother. But this man said nothing at all, and there is a silence and there is a silence. His blanket had been folded back by someone with triage in their hands. One entry wound below the right shoulder blade, small, deceptively neat, a splinter's mouth and not a bullet's; no exit. The chest on that side was not moving with the other. His abdomen was a plank. Under her palm his skin was cold as the stretchers and his lips were the blue of old ink, and when she pressed the plank and let go it stayed pressed.

"Blood pressure?"

The German sister had a cuff and a stethoscope and the look of a woman doing accounts in a falling currency. The number, when it came, was a number that meant hurry.

"Penetrating chest and abdomen." She said it in German, because the hall was German now, and the hall understood German the way a field understands sleet — impersonally. "Spleen is intact or he'd be gone. Liver, I should think, and gut. He needs a table inside twenty minutes or you may all save your paperwork."

"He is a Rittmeister." Vogel said it the way men cite a rank, not a man, but she heard the sentence underneath the sentence, the one that had put this man on her trestle and her name on his chart: the German wing's remaining table was Wendt's, and Wendt was deep in the gunner, and their own Polish surgeon was a woman whose father's name was in Polish papers, and rank had looked at the alternatives and chosen her the way you choose a ferry — not because you trust it but because it is the one that is crossing. "The family name carries weight in Berlin. He comes well recommended by surviving."

"Then he shall have the best of Polish luck," she said, and was already turning, and heard behind her the half-second pause with which a Württemberger decides not to have heard a thing.

The German sister read the tag into the record as they moved him: name, unit, wound, the barn station's pencilled hours — *dressed 13.40, morphia 14.15, morphia 17.00* — a little biography of failing, each entry a rung down a ladder, and Sylwia did the sums without wanting to: eight hours on a barn floor with a hole in his diaphragm, gut contents gone quietly into his peritoneum the whole while, a pulse that had spent eight hours spending itself. Most men were already dead at this point in such a story, and would stay so, and no one would ever count them; this one was about to be an exception, and the exception would be made by her, tonight, in the next forty minutes, or not at all. She had held a great many people's lives in her hands by now and had stopped noticing the weight of the holding; it came back on her tonight only as a kind of anger — no. As a kind of attention. She corrected herself the way one corrects a chart. Attention. The lift groaned them upward past the chapel's cold vaulting.

They ran him up in the lift, which was the hospital's one unambiguous luxury, a cage of riveted iron that the Germans had requisitioned along with everything else and which groaned like a man asked to carry more than a man. She rode beside the stretcher with two fingers on his wrist, because you ride with a finger on the wrist; his pulse came up the shaft of her fingerbones, quick, thready, a weasel of a pulse, running so hard to stand still. In the cage's yellow light his face for the first time arrived as a face and not a wound: young-old, thirty maybe, thirty-two, dry-lipped, jaw clamped on the pain in a way she recognised from other men who had been trained to be ashamed of it. Blackened stubble. Hands like a rider's, the callus laid along the inside of the fingers where the reins live. A wedding ring? No. A signet, no ring. The corps patch on his shoulder had been cut away by whoever had dressed him on the barn station — cut away neatly, the cavalry's death's-head gone to the scissors, and she thought, distantly, that somewhere east of Warsaw a man with good hands had done that scissor-work with love or with fear, and there was no telling which, and it did not matter tonight.

The theatre was warm. It was always the warmest room in the hospital, a fact she had never once in three and a half years taken for granted: warm meant light, and light meant the generator, and the generator meant that the occupying power had decided her theatre mattered. The great lamp was already lit. Danuta was scrubbing at the basin, elbows high, a slight Warsaw girl with a scrub-brush temper and a jeweller's hands, and Ilse, the German sister from the other wing, had come over with the anaesthetic trolley as arranged the way such things were arranged, by a note passed at supper and countersigned by a nod.

The mask went over the quiet man's face. The sweet reek of ether took the room.

"Count backward from ten," Ilse said, in German, kindly, the way she said everything.

He was past counting. His eyes, which had opened once — dark eyes, annoyed, the eyes of a man interrupted mid-sentence by the whole twentieth century — found the lamp, found the ceiling, found, for a moment, nothing at all; and the breathing went over to the machine of the mask, and Sister Ilse's hand on the flask took over the keeping of him, and that was the last thing the night allowed to be simple.

Sylwia held her own two hands up inside the light, fingers spread, the way her teacher Wierzbicki had taught her to begin every operation: *look at them first, doktoro; they are the only instrument that cannot be boiled.* The left, retrained out of its nature. The right, obedient. Steady.

She put out her hand for the knife.

* * *

The prep had been done by orderlies under Danuta's eye while the ether was taking: the whole front of him shaved and painted with iodine, brown-gold from collarbone to groin, the smell of it climbing over the ether the way a tide climbs a beach. It was the paint that did it. It always would be, afterwards; whenever she smelled iodine for as long as she practised she would be back in this minute.

Her eyes went over the field the way they went over every field, cataloguing. The entry wound's dressing, off now, the small dark mouth of it. The chest wall. The nipple line. And on the left side, contralateral, below the lowest ribs, where the paint had thinned across an older territory of skin — a scar. Long. White, the white of skin that has been skin for a long time. A clean oblique draw, six centimetres, following the line a blade takes when it is drawn rather than thrust, and closed by someone who could suture, because it had healed flat and even, seam inside seam, the work of a hospital and not a kitchen.

Her gloved hand went to it before she had decided anything. Two fingers, light, reading it the way she had read forty bellies that night. The glove lied; the glove always lied; it was the flesh beneath the glove that remembered — and the flesh remembered a gymnasium floor in March, the particular sound a sabre makes when it breaks at the forte, a sound like a word said wrong, and the longer sound after it, the one that had gone through the whole hall and out through the high windows into the Berlin morning: not her voice. His. And hers under it, shut behind her teeth, where it had lived for thirteen years, unsaid, unconfessed, unforgiven — the half-second, the unforgivable half-second, when what she had felt before the horror arrived was gladness.

The floor of the theatre did not tilt. That was the strange thing, that was the thing she would take out and look at for the rest of her life: the floor did not tilt, the lamp did not waver, the weasel pulse went on running under Sister Ilse's flask, and her hands stayed exactly where hands stay. Inside her, in the place where Sylwia Kotarska kept her self, a door she had painted over in 1933 opened quietly, the way doors open in dreams, without weight.

"Danuta." Her voice came out with the flatness she used for orders, and she was grateful to it, whoever had built it. "Give me his book."

The Soldbuch went from pocket to trolley to glove. Danuta opened it, because a scrub nurse reads what the surgeon is too busy to read; her finger ran down the lines of type, and Sylwia watched that finger as if the whole night had narrowed itself to one finger moving under electric light.

"Schoeler," Danuta read. "Ludwig. Rittmeister." The rank, the unit — a reconnaissance squadron, the east, the numbers that meant nothing and everything — the birthdate. Danuta, who was nineteen—twenty-three and Warsaw to the bone and had no reason on this earth to be gentle, added the third line of it, the one that was not printed: "Kotarska, pan doktoro. It says he is thirty-two."

"It doesn't say that in the book," Sylwia said. "Give me the knife."

She cut.

She had performed this operation on other men, and the other men had been other men. Now the knowledge of who lay under her hands arrived in the body before it arrived anywhere, a low hum in the sheeted weight of him, in the fact that this breathing was *his*, this opened abdomen was the boy's and then the young man's and then the officer's, the three of them folded inside the uniform like papers in a file. Ludwig Schoeler. Twenty years of that name in every declension hatred could supply — *der Preuße, der Schwimmer, Schoeler, Kotowska*, the two syllables he had always put on her name like a boot on a hand — and now the name lay on her table with a splinter three centimetres from the aorta and his pulse running like a sentry who has heard something in the dark.

There were three seconds available to her. She knew it the way you know the exits of a room you grew up in. Three seconds, no more, in which a hundred small choices sat waiting to be made by nobody: a clamp that slips, a pack that leans, a ligature remembered too late — three seconds that no tribunal on earth could distinguish from fatigue, and the city outside was full of tribunals that would not even ask. He had been nearest. Three seconds was what he had had, and he had spent them on his own friend.

She felt them pass. She counted them out against her own heartbeat, the way you pay out rope, one, two, three, the whole length of her hatred paid across the sheeted body between her hands, and it went through her like cold water and came out the other side having changed nothing, having changed everything, because the hands — the hands had no country. The hands had been boiled and gowned and answerable to the lamp since before she had a name for what was in her. The hands went on.

The abdomen came open on old blood, the smell of a hold of a ship. Suction. Packs, white to brown. Then the liver, which was worse than the splinter, because the splinter was finished and the liver was not: its edge torn where the splinter had printed its signature through the diaphragm, bleeding with the lazy, catastrophic patience of the liver, a darkness welling in the wound's corners like ink under a blotter. She sewed it with deep mattress sutures over a pad, each one a wager that the capsule would hold the thread, and while she sewed the transfusion set's drops came faster at Ilse's adjustment, and at the second flask the man's body, being asked to accept a stranger's blood, shuddered once from the temples down — a long rigor that moved the whole draped field, the liver sagging against her sutures, Danuta's hands already there with the packs before Sylwia had spoken.

"Chill," Ilse said, needlessly, professionally; her stethoscope had heard it before her eyes did. "The flask is warm again in five minutes. The first one is in. Should I stop?"

"Slow it to a trickle and finish the flask." She put her two hands flat on the liver, one over the sutures, and held them there through the shuddering the way you hold a bird that has struck a window, feeling the organ's enormous grievance come back down to a working stillness. It was an era of canned blood, nobody remembered to say so, but it was: strangers' red cells in quart jars, the century had got this far. Somewhere in Wittenau or Tempelhof a donor had sat in a chair in February and been paid in marks or in coupons, and the blood had travelled a thousand kilometres in a lorry to be chilled on a barn station and warmed in a Warsaw theatre and shaken into this German body by the two hands of a Polish surgeon; there was no flag in the transaction anywhere; the red cells would not salute.

Above them, at the head of the table, the two nursing cultures met every night over one man's breathing, Danuta's clips and Ilse's flask, Polish forceps on German rubber, and neither had ever once been heard by Sylwia to say a word against the other. It was one of the war's private jokes, that the only true armistice in Europe ran between a scrub nurse and a Red Cross sister across the width of an operating table. Above it, the splinter itself sat in muscle like a coin dropped in a queue: she took it with the forceps, eased it, turned it in the light for the record — a ragged piece of a railway, iron that had been part of somebody's track bed that morning — and Danuta dropped it into the kidney dish where it rang once and settled, and that was the loudest sound in the room for some seconds, that small bell of spent metal.

The diaphragm had a hole in it the size of a thumb. Through it, when he breathed — when the flask breathed for him — the right lung's lower edge came down like something looking out of a window. She sewed the diaphragm with chromic, unhurried, each stitch a word in a language with no word for *enemy* in it. Then the colon, which the splinter had mauled at the hepatic flexure, its contents already out in the peritoneum doing their slow lethal accounting: that was a loop to be exteriorized, brought out through a separate opening and anchored on a glass rod, an honest wound that would announce its state daily to whoever kept him — there would be no hiding from that one, for either of them. She did it with a craftsman's displeasure. The packing came out brown and went in white. Two flasks of group O stood ready, canned blood from the German service, and the transfusion set ticked its drops like a clock with a limp, and under the ether he endured the whole inventory of her skill with nothing but that weasel pulse for a voice.

Somewhere in the second hour her mind went where minds go, down the lift-shaft of the self, and stood for a moment in a school gymnasium in Berlin-Dahlem with a broken sabre cooling in her fist, a line of white on a boy's flank welling red through a white jacket, and the hall going silent, and the half-second's gladness already gone, already turned to its opposite, already ash — and she put the memory down the way she would have put down a hot instrument, without ceremony, and returned to the liver.

"Count," she said, at the end, to Danuta.

"Four packs, six sponges, one needle, one rod. Knife, forceps" — the jeweller's voice ticking through the tray — "complete."

She sewed him closed in layers, the peritoneum, the sheath, the skin, her stitches small and even as type. The colostomy she dressed last, around the glass rod, with a fenestrated pad and a deliberate tenderness, because whatever else the coming weeks held, the man would wake with an opening in his right flank where his body's dignity used to be, and some other pair of hands — hers, a fortnight of hers — would be the ones he had to look at while he learned to live with it. She had signed her work in his body the way masons sign undersides of keystones, where no one looks until the weight is on it. On the last knot her right hand stopped, one instant, above his painted sternum, because it was done, because for the first time in three hours there was nothing left to do — and the instant held all the weight that three hours of work had kept at bay. Then it passed. Powder for the wound at the back, a drain down into the chest and out under water seal, a dressing white as a first page.

Ilse took the flask off and listened with her stethoscope and nodded, satisfied, and Sister Ilse being satisfied was the most German sound in the building. Vogel appeared at the door in his overcoat with the cold still on him, and looked at the table, and looked at her, and said, in the tone of a man confirming a delivery, "You have given him his life back, Doktor Kotarska."

"I have given him a colostomy," she said, stripping her gloves, "a drain, and forty-one stitches. The life is his own business."

Vogel's face did the thing Württembergers' faces did instead of smiling. "The family name carries weight," he said again, as if the sentence were a receipt he needed countersigned. "Berlin. Schoeler. You would know it, perhaps. A name of judges."

The floor of the world, which had held so beautifully all night, turned once, precisely, the way a needle turns. She kept her eyes on the knot she was tying on the drain.

"I keep my reading in Polish, Herr Oberstabsarzt," she said.

* * *

The tap water ran cold over her hands for a long time, longer than surgical, and she let it, because there are things hands must be made to remember and things hands must be made to forget and running cold water is the border post between them. Under the blood, under the soap, under the iodine ghost on her forearms, her hands were thirty-two years old and had been hands of a surgeon for nine years, hands of a girl with a sabre for thirteen before that. She washed, and the hot room breathed at her back, and in the corridor beyond the glass a night hospital did its hospital sounds — trolleys, boots, the lift, someone laughing far off in the German corridor with the laugh strangled down to regulation volume.

Halina was waiting in the changing room with two glasses of tea the colour of misspent youth and the particular stillness of a friend who has already asked two orderlies and a porter and constructed a story and does not believe one word of it. She was twenty-seven and had a face made for conspiracy, bright and narrow, and the war had not managed to talk her out of her opinions, only taught her to keep them in a drawer with the ammunition.

"Well?" Halina said.

"Well, what."

"You went into that theatre at twenty to eleven and I heard you come out of it at two. Janek says you were four hours with one man. Vogel stood in the corridor with a face on him like a christening." She pushed a glass across. "Who is he?"

"A Rittmeister," Sylwia said. "Cavalry. His train found a mine."

"You know what I am asking."

"A Rittmeister," Sylwia said again, and drank the tea, which was terrible, and looked at her friend with the flat friendly surfaces she had been polishing all her life for exactly this use. "A German officer who is alive, Halina, because I spent four hours making him so. That is the whole of what I know, and the whole of what there is."

The lie sat in her stomach alongside the tea, heavier than the tea. In four years of friendship, since Halina had come to the ward a probationer with a dead fiancé at Młociny and a tongue that had decided not to be consoled, Sylwia had lied to her about three things, and each had been a stone she could still find in herself by touch. This one she would have to carry in a pocket, reachable. There were people, the war kept teaching her, to whom she owed the truth and could not pay it; the currency was not hers; she had stolen the denomination from the Germans. Halina looked at her for a long moment with the bright eyes doing bright-eye things, and then, mercifully, gloriously, the air-raid alarm did not sound and the war did not come through the wall, and Halina said only, "Four hours. God." — and drank her own tea, and began, in the way of the ward's true aristocracy, to complain about the laundry.

On the way down she had gone through the admission hall, because it lay on her path and because forty-one stretchers have a way of leaving stragglers, and found the Polish railwayman from the door sitting where Janek had installed him, boot off, foot wrapped in somebody's muffler, white but no longer grey. His mate sat with him holding both their caps. The German book had no line for them; they would be sent back to the siding with a chit, or not even a chit, and the foot would mend or not in a room somewhere in Wola with the wind in it. She left word at the desk that the man was to be X-rayed when the apparatus was free — knowing, as she left it, that the apparatus belonged to the German wing and its nights belonged to Germans; but the word went into the world, it took up its little room on a slate in the duty room, *the railwayman, the foot*, and a hospital was a country that sometimes answered. She kept accounts like that, small entries, in a ledger no one audited.

Later — it was four, it was five; the ward windows had gone from black to the colour of dirty snow, which in February is what Warsaw means by dawn — she stood at the drawer in her office with the candle lit again, because the generator was given to the theatres and not to prose. The drawer's top tray held the office's honest papers. Under it, under a false bottom made of the drawer's own geometry and a 1939 calendar, lay her other papers, her real ledger, the private archive of the house of Kotarski: her mother's last letter, a lock of Stefan's hair that she was not sentimental enough to have cut and not strong enough to have burned, Jerzy's snitch-file, and — flat, grey-green, gone soft at the folds — a carbon copy of a requisition order dated the sixteenth of September, 1939, designating the Wolska hospital a Wehrmacht lazarett with immediate effect, under the signature *L. Schoeler, Rittmeister.*

She had stolen it from Reychman's filing cabinet in the October of that year, telling herself she was after morphine tallies.

She had been in the administration corridor on a Tuesday, with a snow that was nearly rain coming down outside, and the cabinet had been unlocked because the occupation's first gift to Polish institutions was the conviction that terror made locks redundant. She could still summon the yard if she let herself: the horses stamping, steam off the animals and off the men, the mounting block, a detail of Orpo police with their rifles slung the wrong way for work and the right way for standing about, and Reychman in the doorway with his hands out in the gesture that meant *gentlemen, please, there are typhus wards*, and the officer of the detail already walking the rows of window glass and choosing which floor his office would have. The mounted man had come last, out of the gate arch, riding a bay with a white stocking on its near hind, and she had understood from the Orpo detail's spines rather than anything said whose side procedure was on. He did not raise his voice. He produced from his map case a form — the occupation's true sacrament — and filled it against his saddle, and the shooting detail that had been forming in the yard folded itself away like a camp bed. He never once looked at her, the small woman in the bloodstained apron with the clipboard; she had been one of the building's fixtures to him, and that was why she was alive to hold the memory. The carbon had come to Reychman's cabinet because carbons come to cabinets, and in October she had been at the cabinet, and the key had been in the lock, and the rest was the story she had lived inside for four years.

Four years, and she had never decided what the paper was. A mercy: it had stood between the yard and the firing detail, and things stood in yards that September only when something was written and signed and waved like a flag of a better jurisdiction. A procedure: the squadron had wanted the building, and saving the patients was a by-product, the way a wall keeps out the wind without meaning to. An accident of war: the commonest species of good deed in the occupied provinces, and the only one that survives.

She had not connected the signature to the boy with the sabre scar in thirteen years of keeping the paper. That was the lie she had lived in, she saw now, holding the carbon to the candle's light — thirteen years of a filing system in which Schoeler the name and Schoeler the signature had lived in separate drawers of the same desk, the way the woman who operated and the woman who had been glad for half a second in 1930 had lived in the same body, on either side of a painted door.

She put the paper back. She locked the drawer with the little brass key that lived on the same ring as the ward keys, pendulum and key, two times.

Upstairs, on the German corridor, Sister Ilse had put the chart at the bed's foot where the surgeons' charts went, and the bed stood by the window with the frost doing its white ferns up the glass. The ward was not her parish; Polish surgeons did not round on the German wing; every step of this was irregular, and she took the steps anyway, quietly, in flat shoes, past the sleeping men and the one awake man who watched her without curiosity, because being awake at five in a lazarett teaches you that anything might be happening and none of it is yours.

He lay the way the badly wounded lie, flattened by their own rescue, the mask off now, the oxygen tucked aside, his face turned a few degrees toward the window and the grey coming up over the roofs of Wola. Alive. She stood at the foot of the bed with the chart in her hand and read what she had written eight hours ago in a hand as steady as type, and then she came around the bed, and took his wrist out from under the blanket, and put her fingers where the pulse lives.

The crooked nose was the thing. A night of blood and ether and theatre light, and now the grey dawn lay along his face like a level, and there it was, a nose broken once and set by a boy who did not cry for help because the set was part of the boasting, tilted a degree off true, and she stood in the schoolyard of the Realgymnasium in the inflation winter with the pieces of the slate still biting into her palm, thirteen years old, hearing her own heart and the yard silent, hearing — before the headmaster's steps came down the stones — the first snow of that year beginning to fall on Berlin with a sound like the patient turning of ten thousand pages.

His pulse counted itself out under her fingers. Quick still, and light, but no longer a weasel; a pulse with some intention of continuing.

She knew the feel of every pulse in that building: the typhus annex, the Polish beds, the little granary of heartbeats she was paid in nothing to keep. This one now entered the ledger with the others. She noted it the way she noted everything now, had noted everything since a candle stub and a drawer at five in the morning: as a debt.

It beat like a debt paying interest, this pulse, quick and light under her two fingers, and would keep on beating, she understood, at her — at them, at whatever the two of them were now, a surgeon and a signature in the same drawer of the world. She set the wrist down where she had found it, inside the blanket, the way you resheathe a blade.

The ward went on breathing around them. The frost finished one fern on the glass and began another. She wrote the figures at the bottom of the chart, time, rate, the small honest hieroglyphs of the trade, and her hand, checked once against the light, was steady.
