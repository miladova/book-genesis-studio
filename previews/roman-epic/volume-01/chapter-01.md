# Chapter 1: The Shelf

The shelf began to walk before the floor did.

Marcia saw it move in the polished bronze of a cup. The cup stood near the edge of the table, its little oval reflection showing the storeroom behind her: the jars, the reed baskets, the shelf with its dark line of dust along the wall. The shelf slipped a finger's width to the left. A jar knocked against another. The sound was small, almost polite.

She turned.

Gaius kept his head bent over the account board. His stylus made a dry whisper against the wax. He was drawing a line through the number she had just written.

“You have put down seven,” he said.

“There are seven.”

“There are six.”

“There were seven yesterday.”

“Yesterday's jars are not here.”

“They were here when I counted them.”

“Then you counted badly.”

Marcia set the bronze cup on the table, exactly where it had been. “You count as if the thing you count will become obedient.”

“And you count as if it can hear you.”

He smiled without looking up. His hair, which had been cut close with his father's shears, stuck up over one ear. There was flour on his sleeve, though he had not been in the kitchen. It was the kind of detail he would notice about her and hold until a useful moment.

She leaned over the board. Seven notches. Six jars. One jar had been moved, or broken, or eaten by the house. A jar could not be eaten, but a house had ways of consuming what no one admitted to losing.

“Which one?” she asked.

“Which what?”

“Which jar did you see?”

“I saw six.”

“Names, Gaius. A number is not a witness.”

He put down the stylus. “The squat one with the black shoulder. It was behind the grain sacks yesterday. Today it isn't.”

“So it is missing.”

“Or your father sent it out.”

“He would have told me.”

“Would he?”

There it was: not accusation, not yet. Gaius could put an edge on a question without making it look like a blade. Marcia lifted the cup again and turned it so that the small scratch at its foot faced her. The storeroom came back in the bronze, bent and shivering in the curve.

Her father had sent Gaius's father to check the household accounts. That was the adult explanation. The boy came along because he was old enough to add and young enough to be made to carry things. Marcia had been set to count jars because the servants had other work and because her mother said she should learn where the household's provisions went. The two reasons did not account for why Gaius had been in their courtyard since he was small enough to be carried on his father's hip, or why Marcia could never remember the first day he arrived.

He was not a servant. He was not quite a guest. The people in the house made room for him in different ways, according to which of them was looking. Her father's secretary gave him a stool beside the account board; the cook sent him for onions; the porter stopped him at the outer door if Quintus Varia had company. Marcia could pass the porter without being asked where she was going. She had once tested this by walking out and walking straight back in, carrying nothing. The porter had bowed. Gaius had followed a moment later with a basket and been asked three questions.

“You could have said I was with you,” she had told him afterward.

“I was with you.”

“That was not the question.”

He had considered this, then stolen one of the figs from her plate. “It is the answer I have.”

Now he sat in the same room as her, but the account board belonged to his father and the jars belonged to hers. The distinction was easy to explain and harder to live inside. Marcia's mother said a person ought to know which things were theirs before they began to move them. Her father said a person ought to know who would demand them back. Gaius's father said nothing about ownership unless someone else had first named a price.

The household did not run on one rule. It ran on small permissions that changed with the hour. A servant could cross the inner court with hot water and be invisible; if the same person paused to listen, she became a thief. A visitor could sit near the wall and be honored for waiting, unless he waited too long. Marcia had been taught to notice these things as if noticing were a harmless occupation.

The jars were for oil, not water. Their shoulders were dark from years of hands lifting them. The missing one would have been heavy enough that a child could not carry it alone, and small enough that an adult might carry it without asking. Marcia knew the storage room by the smells layered in it: olive oil, old reed, damp earth, the sharp medicinal scent of a jar opened for her mother's salve. She had counted the shelves because her father said a household lost money one careless object at a time. She had counted the servants because her mother said they were not objects, though she was never sure how to put that difference into the account.

Her mother had once said that a room could hold more than one truth. Marcia had been too young to understand, and had asked what the room was made of. Her mother had laughed, then stopped laughing when she saw that Marcia expected an answer.

“Your father sent it out,” Gaius said now, “or someone did.”

“Then someone has changed the account.”

“Maybe.”

“Who?”

He looked at her then. The teasing had gone from his face. “That is not the same question.”

Beyond the storeroom door, the house moved through its morning. A broom whispered over stone. Someone called for water. In the front court, a mule stamped and shook its harness, the bronze rings clicking together. The city made its usual noise through the walls: a seller calling the measure of his fish, a child protesting, the hard crack of a hammer from a shop below the rise. Beyond those sounds, the Orontes was not visible, but Marcia knew its direction as she knew where the sun would be when it reached the cistern court. The city was made of such knowledge. Where water ran, where a wall narrowed, where one household touched another without admitting it.

“Your father will say the jar was never there,” she said.

“My father says what he can prove.”

“He says what keeps his work.”

“So does yours.”

She looked up sharply. Gaius did not lower his eyes. That was one of the things she liked about him and one of the things she had to forgive him for. He could forget that she had not asked to be his equal in every conversation.

“Do you think my father lies?”

“I think all fathers have learned what words cost.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the one I have.”

She wanted to say that he was unfair, because fairness was the word she used when she wanted him to retreat. Instead she reached for the line of figures. The first mark had been cut into the wax too deeply; the stylus had slipped, making a groove that would remain after the board was smoothed. She rubbed the pad of her thumb across it. The wax softened but did not disappear.

Gaius watched the movement. “You will make a hole.”

“Then the board will have a proper place for the number.”

“That is not how numbers work.”

“Perhaps yours.”

The door opened before Marcia could reply. The cook came through sideways, carrying a covered dish against her hip. She had flour on both forearms and a red line across one cheek where she had wiped it with the wrong side of her hand.

“Your mother wants the little jar,” she said to Marcia. “The one with the dark shoulder. I sent it to the east room.”

“For what?”

“For what your mother wants it for.”

“Is there oil in it?”

“There was.”

“Then how can it be in the east room?”

The cook gave her a look that had outlasted three Varia children and would likely outlast the fourth. “Because I carried it there.”

Gaius dipped the stylus in the wax. “There. Six jars, one in the east room.”

“You knew?” Marcia asked.

“I guessed.”

“You guessed correctly.”

“That is one of the benefits of being right.”

The cook set down the dish. She looked at the board, then at Gaius. “Your father said you were to help with the accounts, not make yourself a magistrate.”

“I am only correcting a number.”

“Then correct it and stop admiring the correction.” She lifted the lid from the dish. Steam slipped out, smelling of onions and cumin. “Marcia, your mother is waiting.”

“I'll take it.”

“You'll drop it.”

“Then you take it.”

The cook's mouth bent at one corner. “I am taking it. You can carry the empty dish back if you need to prove your usefulness.”

Marcia's face warmed. The cook had not meant to shame her; that was worse. She looked at Gaius. He was pretending not to have heard, his attention returned to the board. The number seven waited in the wax, too deep to erase cleanly.

“I was trying to find where it went,” Marcia said.

“Now you know.” The cook lifted the dish. “Try not to make a case of every thing that moves.”

After she had gone, Gaius rubbed a thumb over the line Marcia had cut. “You can add it back.”

“It was never gone.”

“From the room, it was.”

“Not from the house.”

“Then the house needs a better account.”

She looked at the door the cook had closed behind her. The little jar had gone to her mother, who might have used the oil for a cracked lip, a burn, a medicine, or something she would not tell Marcia. The jar belonged to the house; its destination belonged to the person who needed it. The distinction did not fit on the board.

Marcia scraped away the seven and wrote six. The deep groove remained under the new number, visible if the light came across the wax.

The mule stamped again. This time the harness did not answer. The silence after it seemed to widen into the court.

Marcia set the stylus down. A little dust, pale as flour, sifted from the upper edge of the door. It drifted into the bronze cup and lay there.

Gaius had already risen. “Did you hear—”

The shelf walked again.

It slid from the wall with an ugly, prolonged scrape. The jars struck together. One struck the edge and turned, its round body rocking, not falling yet. The floor beneath Marcia's feet shifted, and the table gave a sudden lurch that sent the cup spinning. She caught at it. Gaius caught her wrist.

“Down.”

The word came from somewhere low in his chest. For once, it was not a question disguised as something else.

The jar fell. It burst on the floor, and the sound was swallowed by the next one. The room gave a roar that seemed to come from the walls and from under the stone. Marcia was thrown against the table. A bowl dropped from its shelf and broke in white shards. Somewhere in the house, a woman screamed her own name.

Gaius pulled her under the table. She struck her shoulder against the leg. The board above them jumped. A clay jar rolled across the floor, hit the threshold, and split. Liquid ran beneath the table, smelling of oil and bitter herbs.

The house lifted.

For a moment there was no floor, only the sense that the world had decided to throw everything it held. Marcia's teeth met. The table cracked over her head. Gaius's hand covered the back of her neck, a hard warm weight, while pottery burst in the dark around them. Dust filled her mouth. She could not hear the house now, only a great roaring that had no direction.

Then the floor came back.

It came back at an angle. The table slid. Gaius's hand left her neck. Marcia struck the side of the storeroom and went down with a handful of broken reed. The bronze cup spun in a circle, bright, bright, bright, then fell on its side.

The shaking stopped.

She waited for it to begin again. The silence was so complete she heard the oil dripping from the table onto the floor.

“Gaius.”

He was on one knee, his face turned away. He had both hands over his ear. The room was gray with dust. One wall had split from ceiling to floor, the crack narrow at the top and widening below. Through it, she could see the court in a strip: light, a broken shutter, the mule on its side. The animal's legs moved once and stopped.

“Gaius.”

He looked at her. The blood at his temple was thin and red as a drawn line.

“Can you stand?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Do not answer for me.”

He blinked. “Can you stand?”

“Yes.”

It was not exactly true. She got upright by holding the table. Her right shoulder had begun to hurt, and her fingers were shaking, though the rest of her had not agreed to it. The room tilted when she lifted her head. She took the wall for a moment, careful not to put her hand on the crack.

Outside, the house spoke in fragments. A shout. A cough. A voice calling for Helvia. The mule's harness rattled once and went still.

“Your father?” she said.

“Front court. I think.”

“My mother is in the east room.”

“Then go.”

“You go to your father.”

“My father is—” He stopped. There was dust in his hair and one sleeve had torn at the elbow. He looked down at it as if the cloth had answered a question.

The floor made a smaller movement beneath them. The table creaked. A loose tile clicked against the wall.

“Now,” Marcia said.

They crawled through the storeroom door because the way was lower than standing. The passage beyond had filled with grit and broken plaster. A servant girl stood by the doorway, both hands pressed to her mouth. The girl was called Doto. Marcia knew that, though she had never asked her anything beyond where a tray was. Doto's left foot was bare; blood ran across the instep, too much for a cut from a tile.

“Can you walk?” Marcia asked her.

Doto nodded too fast.

Gaius had already knelt. “Where does it hurt?”

She pointed at the foot. Her mouth moved, but no sound came.

“Get her outside,” Marcia said.

“Which outside?”

“The court.”

“The court has a wall down.”

“Then the passage.”

“The passage has a ceiling.”

There was no answer to that. Marcia looked through the split in the wall. The courtyard showed in a strip of bright dust. A beam had fallen across the paving. On the other side of it lay the mule, one eye white, its flank moving shallowly.

They needed to get to the eastern rooms. The passage led there. The courtyard led to the street. The house had become a set of doors, each one asking who could afford to be wrong.

A fresh rumble passed under the stones. Not the same great violence; this was like a cart being dragged far beneath them. Gaius seized Doto under the arms.

“On your good foot,” he told her. “Put your arm over my shoulder.”

She clutched him. He was taller by half a head, though she outweighed him; the three of them stumbled toward the courtyard. Marcia followed, one hand against the wall. At the opening she caught a glimpse of the fallen beam. It had trapped the mule's harness against a stone post. The animal's head lay at an angle. A stable boy was crouched beside it, making a sound with every breath.

“Leave it,” Gaius said, seeing where she looked.

“I wasn't—”

“Marcia.”

The stable boy was called Hanno. He was older than Gaius and had once lifted Marcia onto a wall so she could see over the market crowd. Now he pressed his palms to the beam and tried to move it. The wood did not shift. The mule's body trembled. From the far side of the court, someone was calling for water.

The second rumble came and stopped. Dust rose from the ceiling in a slow veil.

“Take Doto out,” Marcia said.

Gaius looked at her. “And you?”

“I'll get Hanno.”

“You can't lift that.”

“I can tell him to stop.”

“That isn't the same.”

“No. It is what I can do.”

She took Doto's arm from Gaius and shifted it around her own shoulders. The girl was trembling so hard that their teeth knocked. “Go with him. Stay where there are no walls.”

“Marcia—”

“Go.”

He waited for a breath, deciding whether to argue. Then he took Doto's other side. They crossed the courtyard under the cracked lintel. Gaius put his own body between her and the beam as if that could alter the weight of it.

Marcia turned toward Hanno.

“Move,” she said.

He looked at her with wet eyes. “It'll die.”

“It is dying now. You are not.”

He shook his head, a child's refusal in the face of something too large to bargain with. Marcia reached for his sleeve. The cloth was slick with dust. She pulled once, hard. He came away from the beam, but not before the mule gave a low, terrible breath. Hanno turned back. Marcia held his arm until the sound stopped.

Then they ran.

Not far. The passage opened into the front court, and the front court opened toward the street. Men were pushing through the doorway from outside, each with the same idea: that a house might still contain someone worth finding. A woman stood at the threshold with a child in her arms, not one of the household. She kept asking for a man called Philon. No one answered her. Marcia recognized the woman from the oil seller's shop below the rise, though she had never known her name.

Her father was there, his tunic gray with dust, one hand pressed to his mouth. His face looked too old for him. Her mother sat on a fallen chest with one arm around another servant. There was blood at her hairline. The household steward, Menandros, was trying to hold the front door open while people pushed from the street.

“Mother.” Marcia crossed the court and caught Helvia's hand.

“Your shoulder.”

“It is nothing.”

“Do not tell me what it is. Tell me if you can move it.”

Marcia lifted her arm. Pain made the movement stop halfway. Her mother nodded once, as if the answer had been entered into a record.

“Your father?”

“Here.”

“Gaius?”

“With Doto.”

“Good.”

Helvia's fingers tightened around hers. “How many?”

Marcia looked toward the door, where the stranger was still calling for Philon. “How many what?”

“How many of ours are out?”

It was a question Marcia knew how to answer. It gave her something she could count. She turned toward the court. Menandros. Hanno. Doto and Gaius gone through the doorway. The cook. The two younger servants. Her father's assistant. Her mother. Her father. Herself. The doorkeeper? She could not see him. The woman from the oil shop did not belong to them. Neither did the man still trying to get in. The mule had not been a person, but the house had counted it as property, and now someone would have to write it down.

“Eight here,” she said.

“Who is missing?”

“Iason.”

“Where was he?”

“By the front store.”

Helvia looked at her husband. “Quintus?”

He took his hand away from his mouth. There was a line of blood across his palm. “The front store has fallen.”

Marcia looked at the doorway. The front store was not in view. She could see a ragged piece of the street and the pale face of the oil seller's woman. Behind her, the city had lost its straight lines. Dust rose and settled in folds. Somewhere a bronze bell was ringing, though no one seemed to be holding it.

“Iason may have gone out before it fell,” Gaius said from the doorway.

He had returned without Doto. He was breathing hard. One side of his face was streaked with blood and grit.

“Where is she?” Marcia asked.

“At the open ground with my father. She can stand.”

“You left him?”

“He sent me back.”

“Why?”

“To see if you were alive.”

The answer was too plain. Marcia disliked it because she could not do anything with it.

A sound ran through the courtyard—the shifting of stone, a thousand small pieces deciding where to fall. Her father moved first. “Street. Now. Take nothing.”

The instruction made the household hesitate. Each person looked toward what they had been trying to save: a shawl, a cup, a ledger, the sleeping mat rolled by the door. Menandros had one hand on the chest. The cook had turned toward the kitchen. Marcia's mother had not released her hand.

“Nothing,” Quintus said again. “The walls can come down again.”

“Your account board,” Marcia said.

Her father looked at her as if she had asked him to stop the earth. “Leave it.”

“There are names—”

“Leave it.”

Gaius glanced toward the storeroom. The wax board lay somewhere under the table, with the seven marks and the deep groove she had made. It was not a contract. It was not worth a life. She had only wanted to know which jar was missing.

Another crack split the air above them. Everyone flinched. The front lintel shed a strip of plaster. The child in the stranger's arms began to cry.

Marcia put her hand on Helvia's elbow. “Come.”

They moved as one awkward body toward the street. Her father first, turning back to count the household; her mother with her arm around the servant; Marcia behind her, refusing to let the stranger's woman be pushed aside; Gaius last, looking over his shoulder into the house.

“Come,” Marcia said to him.

He stepped through the doorway. As he did, something collapsed behind them with a crash that shook the paving. The household turned to look. The upper part of the storeroom had given way; its wall opened onto the sky, a broken shelf jutting out like a crooked arm. Jars lay in pieces where the table had stood. The bronze cup was gone.

For one moment Marcia thought of the missing jar, black-shouldered, behind the grain sacks. Then she saw the light above the wall, open where the ceiling had been, and understood that the number was no longer the thing that had changed.

The street was full of people going nowhere. A man carried a door on his back. A child walked barefoot between two strangers. Dust coated the olives in a basket, the white hair of an old woman, the wet mouth of a dog. At the end of the lane the houses leaned toward one another, not touching, as if they were waiting to hear which would fall first.

***

The open ground was beyond the next corner, where the lane widened beside a low stone basin. Marcia had passed it many times without seeing how many people could fit there. Now the space was full, and the walls around it seemed too close. People had brought stools, mats, baskets, infants, old parents, the parts of their houses they could carry. A man walked through with his arms spread, ordering everyone to move away from the cracked side of the street. Nobody listened until a strip of wall came down behind him. Then they moved all at once.

The ground underfoot had been packed hard by carts. Dust lay on it in a pale skin, marked by every sandal and bare foot. Someone had set a cooking pot beside the basin. A woman was pouring water into cups from a skin, one cup at a time, and refusing to fill any of them to the rim. People complained and drank anyway.

Gaius's father stood near the basin, one arm held against his ribs. He had a strip of cloth tied around his head, already dark at the edge. When he saw Gaius, he let out a breath that might have been a laugh if the day had been different.

“You came back.”

“You told me to.”

“I told you to look. I did not tell you to come back through a falling wall.”

“The wall had fallen.”

“Then it was an excellent time not to come back through it.”

Gaius touched the blood at his temple. “I was looking for Marcia.”

His father glanced at her. Not with anger. Not with thanks. He measured what it meant that his son had gone into another household's damaged rooms. Marcia recognized the measurement and disliked that she could read it.

“Your arm?” he asked Gaius.

“Fine.”

“Your head?”

“Still here.”

“Good. Keep it.”

His father's hand rose as if to touch the wound, then stopped short of it. Gaius moved half a step nearer. The gesture was smaller than an embrace, and more guarded.

Marcia's mother had found a place on a low block. Her father stood beside her, speaking to Menandros and two men Marcia did not know. The stranger woman from the doorway had sat on the ground with the child in her lap. She had removed one shoe and was examining the child's foot. The boy kept twisting to look at the people around him. He had stopped crying. Dust whitened the damp tracks on his cheeks.

The woman saw Marcia looking. “He was on the stairs when they came down.”

“Your son?”

“My sister's.”

Marcia waited for more. The woman bent over the child and wiped his mouth with the hem of her garment.

“Where is your sister?”

“Looking.”

The word meant the woman did not know. It did not mean the sister was dead. Marcia knew the difference, but the people gathering around them had begun to speak as if only one answer could be endured. Someone said that a certain house had fallen. Someone else said it had not, that a wall had fallen and the house still stood, and that it mattered. A man with dust in his beard called for his brother. A girl answered to the wrong name. The basin water clouded as each cup was dipped.

Her mother called her over. “Help me keep the names.”

Marcia took the board from the servant who had been carrying it. The wax had cracked. One corner had broken off, and the remaining surface was full of scratches from the fall. She pressed her thumb to it, testing the marks. The board could still be used if she avoided the split.

“Who is here?” she asked.

“Those of the household,” Helvia said. She had found a reed stylus in someone's basket. “Then the others, separately.”

“Why separately?”

“Because your father asked for his household.”

“Are they not here too?”

Her mother held her gaze. “They are here. The list is for your father.”

That was not a reason. It was a description of the person who would read it.

Marcia began with those whose names she knew. Her mother, her father, Menandros, the cook, Hanno, Doto—she marked the last name and looked around. Gaius stood by the basin with his father. Both were present, but not of her father's household. She put them on a second line. Then she turned to the woman and the child.

“What is your name?”

The woman looked at the board. “Mine?”

“Both of yours.”

“Melita. He is Theon.”

“Your sister?”

“Callianeira.”

Marcia wrote each name. The stylus caught in the broken edge; she shifted her hand and continued. Her mother watched without stopping her.

“Does she belong to the oil shop?” Gaius asked from behind her.

Melita looked up. “I belong to myself.”

The words were not loud. They carried because the people closest to her had fallen quiet.

Gaius lowered his eyes. “I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

Marcia heard the answer underneath it. In the account, every person belonged somewhere: to a house, a workshop, a family, a debt. That did not mean the person agreed. It meant someone would be asked to answer for them.

“My father wanted the household list,” she said. “You are on the other side.”

Melita looked at the tablet. “If you write us there, will anyone come looking?”

Marcia looked toward the men moving through the crowd, calling names. “I don't know.”

“Then write us where the people are looking.”

Her mother shifted on the block. The cut at her hairline had begun to bleed again. “Marcia.”

“I am writing names, Mother. Not promises.”

“Names become promises when people need them to.”

“Then they should be written correctly.”

She turned the board sideways and made a third line. The stylus dragged at the crack. She wrote Melita, Theon, and Callianeira. Beside the last name she marked no answer. It was not a count of the living, only a record that someone was still expected.

Gaius came to stand beside her. “You have put down three.”

“There are three.”

“Two are here.”

“One is missing.”

“Then you should mark the difference.”

“I did.”

He bent to look. “Not where your father will see it.”

“He will see the line.”

“He'll see a list of people who aren't his.”

“Then he can ask why they are there.”

Gaius's mouth tightened. He reached for the stylus, then stopped before his fingers touched it. For a breath, Marcia expected him to argue. Instead he pointed to the broken corner.

“Your board will split if you press there.”

She moved her hand. The wax held.

Around them, people arranged themselves by the smallest available claims. A family shared a cloak. Two men argued over a basket neither would admit to owning. An old woman sat on a bundle of clothes and told a stranger to move his foot because it was on her son's hand. The stranger moved it. A boy ran from the basin with a mouth full of water, not swallowed but carried there, spilling down his chin. Someone told him he had done well. Someone else told him not to waste it.

No one had decided who was in charge. The man who had been ordering people away from the cracked wall now held a child while the child's mother tied a strip of cloth around her palm. The woman with the water skin had stopped filling cups. She was giving water to anyone who brought an empty vessel without asking their name. Menandros had found a place beside Marcia's father and was counting something in a low voice. Her father answered him with numbers, then stopped when the count did not match.

Marcia's mother closed her hand around the edge of the board. “This is not the household account.”

“No.”

“You cannot make a city from a list.”

“I am not trying.”

“What are you trying to do?”

Marcia looked at the names, each one cut shallow into the wax. The board was cracked; the letters would not last. She had no idea whether Callianeira was alive or whether Melita would leave the open ground to search for her. She did not know who would read the list or what they would do with it. The only thing she could make certain was that, for this moment, the missing woman had a place among the people expected back.

“I am trying not to lose them twice.”

Her mother said nothing. The cut beside her hair had stopped bleeding. She looked tired, but not in a way that invited comfort.

Gaius's father called to him. Gaius went, then turned once to look back. Marcia did not wave. There were too many people watching, and it would have meant nothing to them. He tapped two fingers against his own palm—their old sign for wait, or count again. She answered by pressing the stylus into the wax.

The city continued to shake, though the tremors had grown faint enough that people could pretend not to feel them. A bronze vessel rolled in a slow circle at the edge of the ground. No one tried to catch it. It tipped against a stone and lay still.

Marcia counted the people beside her again.

Her mother. Her father. Menandros. The cook. Hanno. Gaius. The stranger with the child. Eight, if she counted the woman; seven, if she counted only her father's house. She could not decide which number her mother wanted. The tablet had begun to soften beneath her thumb, warmed by the friction of writing. Every mark she made had to be shallow enough to leave room for the next one. The board was not a record of what had happened; it was a record of what they could still ask about.

A boy she did not know approached with a broken sandal in his hand. He was carrying it as if it were a message. He looked from Marcia to the board, then back toward the lane where his house had been.

“Are you counting?” he asked.

“I'm trying.”

“Count me.”

Marcia looked at his feet. One was bare. “What is your name?”

He told her. She wrote it beneath the others, not in the household line and not in the third line, because she did not know where he belonged. The wax took the name anyway.

Gaius wiped the blood from his temple with his sleeve. “How many?”

Marcia looked toward the open ground, where her father's clerk and Gaius's father were calling names into the dust.

“I don't know,” she said.

It was the first answer that day she had not tried to improve.
